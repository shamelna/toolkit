What is queue theory?

Queue theory (or queueing theory) is the mathematical study of waiting lines. It gives us a way to predict how a system behaves when demand is random — how long people wait, how many pile up, and how busy servers are.

Every queue has three core components: an arrival process (how customers enter), a service mechanism (how fast they're served), and a queue discipline (the order they're served in, usually FIFO).

Why it matters in Lean: Queue theory makes waste visible with numbers. It proves that adding capacity is not always the answer — sometimes reducing variability or smoothing arrivals is far more effective.

The Kendall notation

Queuing models are described using Kendall's notation: A/S/c — where A is the arrival distribution, S is the service distribution, and c is the number of servers.

Kendall notation A / S / c / K / N / D A = arrival process · S = service process · c = servers K = capacity limit · N = population size · D = discipline

The M/M/1 model (Markovian arrivals, Markovian service, 1 server) is the simplest and most widely used. The M/M/c model used in this simulator extends it to multiple servers.

The "M" stands for memoryless (exponential distribution) — meaning the probability of the next arrival doesn't depend on when the last one came.

M/M/1 — single server

Traffic intensity ρ = λ / μ Must be < 1 for stability
Avg customers in queue (Lq) Lq = ρ² / (1 − ρ)
Avg customers in system (L) L = ρ / (1 − ρ)
Avg wait in queue (Wq) Wq = Lq / λ
Avg time in system (W) W = Wq + 1/μ = L / λ

M/M/c — multiple servers

With c servers, the offered load per server is reduced. The Erlang-C formula calculates the probability a customer must wait (P_wait):

Server utilisation per server ρ = λ / (c × μ) Stability requires ρ < 1
Erlang-C (prob. of waiting) C(c,a) = (aᶜ/c!) × (1/(1−ρ)) × P₀ where a = λ/μ (offered load)
Avg queue length Lq = C(c,a) × ρ / (1 − ρ)

Key insight: Going from 1 to 2 servers doesn't halve wait time — it can reduce it by 75% or more near ρ = 0.9. The relationship is highly non-linear.

Little's Law

Proposed by John D.C. Little in 1961, Little's Law is one of the most powerful and general results in operations management. It applies to any stable system, regardless of the arrival distribution, service distribution, or queue discipline.

Little's Law L = λ × W L = avg number in system · λ = arrival rate · W = avg time in system

The law also applies within sub-components of the system:

For the queue alone Lq = λ × Wq

Practical applications

Little's Law lets you derive any one of the three variables if you know the other two. This is incredibly useful in practice because arrivals (λ) and queue length (L) are often easy to count, but wait time (W) is hard to measure directly.

You knowYou can findFormula
λ and LW (avg time in system)W = L / λ
λ and WL (avg in system)L = λ × W
L and Wλ (throughput rate)λ = L / W

Lean example: If 10 WIP items sit in a process and throughput is 2/hour, each item waits 5 hours on average (W = 10/2). Reducing WIP to 5 cuts wait time to 2.5 hours — without speeding up the process at all.

Understanding traffic intensity (ρ)

Traffic intensity ρ (rho) is the single most important number in queue theory. It measures the fraction of time a server is busy — equivalently, the ratio of demand to capacity.

Traffic intensity ρ = λ / (c × μ) λ = arrival rate · μ = service rate per server · c = servers

Queue length grows non-linearly with ρ. Near ρ = 1, even a tiny increase in demand causes wait times to explode. This is the "cliff effect" — the core reason why operating at 100% utilisation is so dangerous.

0 — optimal0.75 — caution1.0 — collapse

The utilisation zones

ρ rangeStatusImplication
< 0.30UnderutilisedExcess capacity. Review staffing or absorb more demand.
0.30 – 0.75BalancedOptimal zone. Focus on reducing variance.
0.75 – 0.90High utilisationEfficient but fragile. Plan extra capacity for peaks.
0.90 – 1.00CriticalLong waits. Add capacity or reduce arrivals urgently.
≥ 1.00UnstableQueue grows forever. System will collapse.

Why 85% is often used as a target: At ρ = 0.85, the system has a 15% buffer for variability. Above 0.90 the wait-time curve becomes so steep that normal demand variation causes severe service failures.

Queue theory and Lean thinking

Queue theory underpins many Lean tools — it gives the mathematical proof for principles that Toyota discovered empirically. Understanding the maths helps you make the case for improvement with hard numbers.

Lean principleQueue theory explanation
Reduce WIPLower L → lower W (Little's Law). Less inventory = faster flow.
Level loading (Heijunka)Smoothing λ reduces variance, which dramatically reduces Wq even at the same average ρ.
Reduce batch sizesSmaller batches reduce effective service time variability (σ), cutting queue length.
Standard workConsistent μ (lower σ) reduces Lq non-linearly — variance hurts more than averages.
Pull systems (Kanban)Cap L at a fixed level → cap W automatically → predictable lead times.
Right-size capacityTarget ρ = 0.70–0.85 to absorb demand spikes without queue explosion.

Variability — the hidden enemy

The M/M/c model assumes exponential service times. In reality, variance is often the biggest driver of queuing problems — even when average capacity exceeds average demand.

VUT equation (approximation) Wq ≈ (Ca² + Cs²)/2 × ρ/(1−ρ) × (1/μ) Ca = coefficient of variation of arrivals · Cs = of service times

This shows that reducing variability (Ca or Cs) has the same mathematical effect as adding capacity. A process with consistent, predictable cycle times will always outperform one with higher average throughput but high variance.

Practical implication: Before adding servers, use standard work and mistake-proofing to reduce σ(service time). You may not need extra headcount at all.

When to use which model

The M/M/c model (used in this simulator) is the best starting point for most improvement projects. It gives conservative estimates — real-world systems with lower variance will perform better.

Key assumptions to be aware of:

Poisson arrivals — customers arrive independently and randomly. Works well for walk-in demand but not for scheduled appointments.

Exponential service times — high variance assumed. If your process uses standard work, actual wait times will be lower than the model predicts.

Infinite population — suitable when the total number of potential customers is much larger than the queue. For small populations (e.g. a machine servicing 10 jobs), use the M/M/c/K/N model.

Rule of thumb: Use M/M/c to size for worst case. If real variance is lower than exponential, your system will perform better than the model predicts — which is a safe position to design from.
